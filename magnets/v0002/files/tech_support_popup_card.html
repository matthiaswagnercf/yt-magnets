The Pop-Up Decision Card + the Refund Letter

Built for the Breach Record video on FTC v. Nuvei (D. Ariz. 2:26-cv-06306, complaint filed September 3, 2026). Free.

The card (print it, tape it under the monitor)

If you seeIt meansDo
A full-screen warning that "Windows" or "your system is damaged" with a phone numberNo operating system shows a phone number. The complaint says these pop-ups "were often made to appear as messages from the Microsoft operating system" (para 57).Close the browser (Cmd/Ctrl+W, or restart). Never call.
A "free scan" that finds "critical errors" and asks you to buy a "repair"The complaint: pop-ups "direct consumers to download free software that purported to confirm the fake virus or malware findings" and sell a repair for $30 to $60 (para 58).Do not install. Run only the antivirus you already have. Microsoft's own investigator found "no threats prior to the test event" (para 88).
A support agent who wants to remote into your computerThe Microsoft test purchase: the agent "remoted into the computer, ran some 'tests' and claimed to find critical system errors" and sold a "Platinum" package (para 88).Hang up. Nobody who needs your money should be holding your mouse.
A price that differs from what someone else paid"one consumer was charged $500 for the Platinum package, whereas a different consumer in early 2021 was charged $1,000 for the same package" (para 61).A price that changes with the caller is a script, not a service.
A charge on your card from a company you never heard ofThe processor's contact information appeared on statements in place of the seller's (para 73).Chargeback (below).

The four steps if you already paid

  1. Call the number on the back of your card and open a dispute ("services not as described / deceptive telemarketing"); give the merchant name on the statement and the date.
  2. Uninstall the "repair" software; change the passwords used on that computer since the call, starting with email and bank; turn on two-factor.
  3. Report at reportfraud.ftc.gov (US) or antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca (Canada). Reports are what built this complaint.
  4. If a refund program exists for the seller (the FTC obtained a $26 million judgment against Reimage/Restoro in March 2024), check ftc.gov/refunds. The FTC never charges a fee to send a refund, and never calls asking for bank details to send one.

Chargeback letter (fill the brackets)

Dear [Bank], On [date] my card ending [1234] was charged [amount] by [merchant name on statement]. The charge was for "technical support" sold after a pop-up warning falsely claiming my computer was infected; the seller remotely accessed my computer and sold services I did not need. The Federal Trade Commission has filed a complaint describing this exact practice (FTC v. Nuvei Corp., D. Ariz. No. 2:26-cv-06306, filed September 3, 2026, paragraphs 57-62 and 88). I did not receive the service as described and request a chargeback. [Name, date]

Source: FTC complaint, https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/Nuvei-Complaint.pdf

The refund that already happened (so you recognize the real one)

In March 2025 the FTC sent more than $25.5 million to 736,375 people harmed by Restoro and Reimage, by PayPal, from the $26 million March 2024 judgment. The FTC's own words: "The Commission never requires people to pay money or provide account information to submit a claim or receive a refund." Anyone who calls asking for a fee or your bank login to "release" a refund is the next scam. Sources: ftc.gov press releases of 2024-03-14 and 2025-03-10.
